Shell Eggs Recalled After a Salmonella Outbreak Across 17 States

FDA Salmonella Eggs Published August 4, 2026

Eggs are one of the few foods almost every household has open at any given time — which is exactly what makes an egg recall worth two minutes of your attention.

The FDA is investigating a multistate outbreak of Salmonella Enteritidis linked to shell eggs. A total of 98 people have been infected across 17 states, and the eggs have been recalled by Midwest Poultry Services, L.P.

Why eggs and Salmonella keep meeting

Salmonella Enteritidis has an unusual trick: it can be present inside an intact, clean, uncracked egg, because the bacterium can infect the hen's ovary before the shell ever forms. That's why "the shell looked fine" tells you nothing, and why washing an egg doesn't solve the problem.

The reliable defence is heat. Cooking eggs until both the white and the yolk are firm kills the bacteria. The risk lives in the middle ground most of us like: runny yolks, soft scrambles, and above all the uncooked places eggs hide — homemade mayonnaise, aioli, hollandaise, tiramisu, raw cookie dough, protein shakes with a raw egg in them.

Salmonellosis, briefly

Symptoms usually appear 6 hours to 6 days after eating contaminated food: diarrhea, fever and stomach cramps, typically resolving in under a week. Most people recover without treatment. It hits harder in children under 5, adults over 65, and anyone immunocompromised, where dehydration and invasive infection become real risks.

Checking a carton is easier than it sounds

Egg recalls are identified by information stamped on the carton — plant number, Julian date and best-by date, plus the UPC. It's more legible than a lot code on a bag of lettuce, but it's still small print you'd never think to compare against a federal notice. That mismatch — official data that exists but never reaches the kitchen — is the whole problem with recalls.

What to do

Check your egg cartons against the identifying details in the FDA recall notice. If they match, or you can't tell, don't eat them — and don't just cook them thoroughly and move on: the FDA's advice for recalled eggs is to throw them out or return them, and to wash surfaces, containers and hands that touched them, since Salmonella spreads readily around a kitchen. If you have diarrhea with fever after eating eggs, contact a healthcare provider, especially for a young child or an older adult.
